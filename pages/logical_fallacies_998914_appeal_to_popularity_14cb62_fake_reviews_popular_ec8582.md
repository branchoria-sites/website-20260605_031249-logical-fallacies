## Introduction

Online ratings and reviews can be useful because they summarise the experiences of many people in a form that is easy to compare. The problem is that review systems can be manipulated. When businesses buy reviews, create fake customer accounts, suppress criticism, or inflate popularity metrics, they manufacture the appearance of crowd approval. The resulting signal looks like evidence, but it may reflect marketing tactics rather than genuine consumer [experience]({{ 'experience/' | relative_url }}).

Within the broader appeal to popularity fallacy, fake reviews matter because they create an artificial majority. A shopper who sees thousands of positive ratings may reasonably assume that many independent customers reached the same conclusion. If those signals have been engineered, however, the apparent consensus is no longer reliable evidence. ## How Review Counts Become Evidence Shortcuts

People rarely have time to investigate every purchase in detail. Reviews reduce uncertainty by acting as a form of [social proof]({{ 'social-proof/' | relative_url }}). A high rating, a large review count, or a “best seller” label suggests that many others have already tested the product. Research on online reviews consistently finds that consumers use these signals as indicators of credibility, quality, and trustworthiness. ScienceDirect <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://marketingcourse.org/the-psychology-of-online-reviews-and-ratings-leveraging-social-proof-for-marketing-success/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: marketingcourse.org">[MarketingCourse.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">marketingcourse.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Psychology of Online Reviews and Ratings: Leveraging...5 May 2025 — Online reviews and ratings serve as a potent form of social proo...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2025</span></span></span>

This shortcut is often sensible. If hundreds of independent customers report similar experiences, the pattern may provide useful information. The difficulty is that shoppers usually cannot directly observe whether those reviews are independent, genuine, or representative.

The appeal to popularity fallacy enters when the reasoning becomes:

* Many people appear to approve of this product.
* Therefore the product must be good.

The first statement concerns popularity. The second concerns quality. Reviews can provide evidence about quality, but only if the popularity signal itself is trustworthy. When the signal has been manipulated, the apparent crowd becomes part of the deception rather than a source of evidence.

## Fake Reviews, Bought Praise, and Suppressed Criticism

Manufactured popularity can take several forms.

### Invented Customers

The most obvious form is the fake review written by someone who never used the product or service. Regulators increasingly treat such practices as deceptive [advertising]({{ 'advertising/' | relative_url }}). In 2024, the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) finalised a rule prohibiting businesses from creating, purchasing, selling, or using reviews that falsely represent genuine customer experiences, including reviews generated by non-existent people or artificial intelligence. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Federal Trade Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Trade CommissionThe Federal Trade Commission (FTC) is an independent agency of the United States government whose principal mi...</span></span></span> <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Federal Trade Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Trade CommissionThe Federal Trade Commission (FTC) is an independent agency of the United States government whose principal mi...</span></span></span>

### Paid or Incentivised Endorsements

Some schemes involve real people who are rewarded for leaving positive feedback. The resulting review may not be completely fabricated, but the incentive can distort what is written. The visible popularity signal then reflects a marketing campaign as much as genuine customer satisfaction. ### Review Suppression

Manufactured popularity is not limited to adding praise. It can also involve removing criticism.

## Why Manufactured Popularity Is So Persuasive

Fake reviews succeed because they exploit a normally useful psychological habit.

When people lack direct knowledge, they often look to others for guidance. In many situations this works well. A large number of independent judgements can reveal useful information. The challenge is that consumers cannot easily distinguish between a genuine crowd and a manufactured one.

Modern review systems also compress complex information into simple metrics:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* Average star ratings.
* Total review counts.
* Numbers of followers or subscribers.
* "Most popular" rankings.
* Trending lists and recommendation badges.

</div>

These indicators are attractive because they simplify decisions. Yet the simpler the signal, the easier it can be to manipulate. A shopper may never read individual reviews and instead rely entirely on a numerical summary that can be artificially inflated. ## Practical Signs That Popularity May Be Manufactured

No single warning sign proves that reviews are fake. However, several patterns can justify closer scrutiny.

### Unusually Uniform Praise

A product with thousands of reviews and almost no criticism deserves a second look. Real customers usually disagree about at least some aspects of a product.

### Repetitive Language

Large numbers of reviews using similar wording, identical phrases, or highly generic praise can indicate coordinated activity rather than independent experiences.

### Sudden Review Surges

An abrupt spike in reviews over a short period may reflect a promotion campaign, a review-buying operation, or other artificial activity.

### Mismatch Between Reviews and Details

A large review count attached to a recently launched product, or reviews that discuss features not present in the current item, may indicate review recycling or catalogue abuse.

### Missing Specific Experience

Genuine reviews often mention concrete details about use, delivery, performance, or limitations. Reviews that remain vague while offering strong praise can be less informative.

## What Fake Reviews Teach About Appeal to Popularity

Fake reviews are a practical demonstration of why popularity alone cannot establish truth.

The persuasive force of a five-star rating comes from an implicit assumption: many independent people reached similar conclusions after real experience. Once that assumption is weakened, the popularity signal loses much of its evidential value.

This does not mean reviews are useless. Ratings and reviews can provide valuable information when they reflect authentic customer experiences and when platforms actively detect manipulation. The mistake is treating popularity itself as proof rather than asking how that popularity was produced.

The broader lesson mirrors the central problem of appeal to popularity. A crowd may be correct, mistaken, manipulated, copied from a common source, or entirely manufactured.

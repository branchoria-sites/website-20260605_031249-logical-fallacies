## Introduction

Regression to the mean is one of the most common hidden explanations behind persuasive [post hoc]({{ 'post-hoc/' | relative_url }}) stories. When an outcome is unusually good or unusually bad, the next measurement is often closer to average even if nothing important has changed. As a result, an intervention introduced after an extreme result can appear effective simply because improvement was already likely. This creates a powerful form of false-cause reasoning: a treatment, management decision, motivational speech, policy change, or training programme receives credit for a recovery that would probably have happened anyway. Statistical researchers have long warned that regression to the mean can make ordinary variation look like genuine change, especially when attention is focused on unusually extreme cases.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15333621/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Regression to the mean: what it is and how to deal with itby AG Barnett · 2005 · Cited by 2448 — Regression to the mean (RTM) is a...</span></span></span><span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/34/1/215/638499" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicRegression to the mean: what it is and how to deal with itby AG Barnett · 2005 · Cited by 2470 — Regression to the mean (RTM)...</span></span></span>

Within the broader family of post hoc fallacies, regression to the mean matters because it offers a specific alternative explanation. ## Why Extreme Results Tend to Soften Over Time

Regression to the mean occurs when measurements are influenced by both stable factors and temporary fluctuations. An extreme score often reflects a mixture of genuine underlying conditions and short-term influences such as luck, timing, measurement error, fatigue, stress, or random variation. Because those temporary influences are unlikely to repeat in exactly the same way, later measurements tend to move closer to the long-run average. OUP Academic<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://statisticsbyjim.com/basics/regression-to-the-mean/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: statisticsbyjim.com">[Statistics By Jim]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">statisticsbyjim.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">regression to the mean</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistics By JimRegression to the Mean: Definition &amp; ExamplesMar 28, 2024 — Regression to the mean is the statistical tendency for an un...</span></span></span>

A classic example involves [test scores]({{ 'test-scores/' | relative_url }}). A student who performs extraordinarily well on one test may have benefited from unusually favourable circumstances. Another student who performs extraordinarily badly may have suffered from temporary disadvantages. On a later test, both students are likely to move somewhat closer to their typical level of performance. This does not require any intervention. It is a consequence of how variation works when outcomes are not perfectly consistent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_toward_the_mean" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Regression toward the mean</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Regression toward the mean</span></span></span>

The key point is that regression to the mean is statistical rather than causal. It does not mean that some force is pushing outcomes back to average. Instead, extreme observations often contain a larger component of chance than ordinary observations, making repetition of the same extreme result unlikely.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://statisticsbyjim.com/basics/regression-to-the-mean/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: statisticsbyjim.com">[Statistics By Jim]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">statisticsbyjim.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">regression to the mean</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistics By JimRegression to the Mean: Definition &amp; ExamplesMar 28, 2024 — Regression to the mean is the statistical tendency for an un...</span></span></span><span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.abc.net.au/science/articles/2015/05/12/4217146.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News What does regression to the mean, mean?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What does regression to the mean, mean?May 12, 2015 — Regression is caused by performances fluctuating about ability, so that performance...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 12, 2015</span></span></span>

## How Regression Creates False Success Stories

The most misleading situations arise when people act only after a result becomes extreme.

Imagine a manager intervenes after a sales team records its worst month in years. The next month's figures improve. The manager concludes that the intervention worked. Yet if the poor month was partly an outlier, some improvement may have been expected even if the manager had done nothing.

## Health: Why Patients Often Improve After Seeking Treatment

Health care provides some of the clearest examples because patients frequently seek help when symptoms are at their worst.

A person suffering recurring pain, migraines, depression, allergies, or other fluctuating conditions is more likely to book an appointment during a severe episode than during a mild one. If symptoms naturally fluctuate, improvement may occur afterwards even without an effective treatment. Researchers have identified regression to the mean as a major challenge when evaluating therapies, particularly in studies that examine only patients before and [after treatment]({{ 'after-treatment/' | relative_url }}) without adequate controls.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695861/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCCombined bias suppression in single-arm therapy studies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by HJ Hamre · 2008 · Cited by 44 — Regression to the mean from self-selection at symptom peaks can be suppressed in three ways: by usi...</span></span></span><span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2695861/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCCombined bias suppression in single-arm therapy studies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by HJ Hamre · 2008 · Cited by 44 — Regression to the mean from self-selection at symptom peaks can be suppressed in three ways: by usi...</span></span></span>

## Business: The Illusion of the Successful Intervention

Businesses frequently reward or punish employees after unusually good or bad performance. This creates fertile ground for post hoc stories.

Suppose a sales representative has a terrible quarter. Management introduces a new coaching programme. The next quarter is better. The programme is celebrated as a success.

However, the same logic applies to exceptional success. A representative who achieves an extraordinary quarter may receive a special reward, only to perform less spectacularly afterwards. Observers may conclude that praise reduced motivation. Sport offers vivid examples because extraordinary performances attract attention.

The famous “Sports Illustrated cover jinx” claims that athletes featured on the magazine's cover often decline afterwards. Since exceptional performances are difficult to sustain, subsequent results are likely to be less extraordinary. ## How to Spot Regression in a Post Hoc Claim

Several warning signs suggest that regression to the mean may be operating behind a causal story:

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The intervention followed an unusually extreme result.**
* **The evidence relies mainly on before-and-after comparisons.**
* **The outcome naturally fluctuates over time.**
* **No comparable control group is available.**
* **The improvement is measured immediately after a peak or trough.**
* **The explanation assumes that any subsequent change must have been caused by the intervention.**

</div>

When these conditions are present, a post hoc explanation deserves caution. Improvement may be real, but the observed change alone cannot establish why it happened. ## Why This Matters for Logical Reasoning

Regression to the mean does not prove that an intervention failed. Sometimes treatments, policies, coaching methods, or management decisions genuinely work. The lesson is narrower but important: improvement after an intervention is not sufficient evidence that the intervention caused the improvement.

In post hoc reasoning, people naturally focus on what changed most recently. Regression to the mean reminds us to examine what was already likely to happen. When an outcome begins from an extreme position, movement towards a more ordinary result may require no special cause at all.
